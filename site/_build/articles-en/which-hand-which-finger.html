Which hand, which finger: the customs explained

Published 19 August 2026 · 5 min read · by the Oneiro house

The question seems trivial until the day you marry someone from another country. Or until an aunt points out, over dinner, that "you don't wear it on that side". Here are the customs, where they come from, and the only rule that really matters.

The left ring finger, and the legend behind it

In France, Belgium, the United Kingdom, Italy, the United States: the ring finger of the left hand, for the engagement ring as for the wedding band. The traditional justification is Roman: a vein, the vena amoris, was said to run directly from that finger to the heart.

It is anatomically false — every finger is supplied the same way. But the custom held for two thousand years, which says a great deal about the power of a good story. The likeliest explanation is more prosaic: in a mostly right-handed population, the left hand damages a piece of jewellery less.

The countries that wear it on the right

The list is longer than people think: Germany, Austria, Poland, Russia, Norway, Denmark, Greece, Spain (depending on the region), along with much of Orthodox Europe and Latin America. In Germany the custom even has the engagement ring worn on the left before the wedding, then moved to the right on the day.

In binational couples there are two solutions: pick a side, or wear a ring on each. We have seen both, and the second has its charm.

On the wedding day: the order of the bands

Tradition has the wedding band placed first, closest to the heart — so beneath the engagement ring, on the palm side. Many take the engagement ring off on the morning of the ceremony, wear it on the right hand during the exchange, then put it back over the band in the evening.

It is a lovely detail, it is optional, and nobody will hold it against you if you do the opposite. What matters more: that the two rings sit together without scratching each other or turning. We cover that in the wedding band guide.

And the other fingers?

Nothing forbids leaving the ring finger, and more and more people do. The index carries graphic, assertive pieces well. The middle finger suits wide bands. The little finger remains the domain of the signet ring. A ring like IRIA, with its oval and trio of bands, wears very well away from the ring finger — and many of our clients choose it precisely as a ring for themselves, with no occasion attached.

Some would rather wear no ring at all: manual work, gloves, allergies, or simple preference. The symbol then moves without losing anything: a LYRA pendant worn close to the heart, or a pair of LÉNA studs put on every morning. It is a request we receive regularly, and it deserves better than a raised eyebrow.

Engraving: what people actually put

The date, initials, a short phrase. Three quarters of our clients choose the date; the finest texts are the briefest. Allow around twenty comfortable characters on a standard width band, more on a wide one. Inside engraving is included on our wedding bands.

The only rule that matters

None of these customs carries any weight beyond the weight you give it. What matters is that the ring gets worn: that it does not bother you, does not catch, and does not have to come off to live. That is a design criterion, and it is our job. The rest belongs to the aunts.
